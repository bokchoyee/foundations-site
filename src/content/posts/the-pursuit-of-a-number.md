We all have goals in our lives that are tied to certain metrics, and those metrics are used to symbolize success and achievement. High grades in school are meant to reflect consistent learning and academic growth. A larger bank balance signals financial stability and success. These measurable indicators are necessary to simplify complex ideas into something we can easily understand and evaluate. While these metrics can help us understand what success means to us, they can also gradually reshape what success truly means to us.

A contradiction surrounding school made me notice this. Universities use grades to identify capable students because strong academic performance is associated with discipline, commitment, and consistent learning. As students, we pursue those high numbers because admission into a university provides opportunities that can influence our future careers and lives. Yet, sometimes the pursuit of these numbers overshadows the purpose that they were meant to serve. The learning, curiosity, and understanding that the grades were meant to represent can become less important than the numbers themselves. Instead of the grades reflecting the learning, the learning starts revolving around the grades.

This pattern not only arises in school settings. Money is often used to provide a sense of flexibility, freedom, and security, yet people can start treating the accumulation of wealth as the goal itself. Leadership positions are meant to recognize meaningful contribution, but titles can sometimes outweigh the importance of the impact that they were supposed to reflect. In both of these situations, the things we value and the things we eventually end up pursuing aren't always identical.

A lot of these goals are easy to gravitate towards because they are easy to look at. Titles and numbers are visible, measurable, and easier to compare. It's difficult to quantify how much you genuinely have learned about a topic, whether in school or not. Contribution and effort are usually subjective. The meaning of success can vary depending on the person. These metrics simplify this uncertainty and complexity into something real and concrete. As a result, the easiest thing to measure often becomes the easiest thing to pursue.

That being said, this doesn't mean that having metrics themselves is inherently bad and should be avoided entirely. Universities need a practical way to judge thousands of applications annually. Employers need a way to reward qualifications and experience. Having financial goals provides a clear direction and encourages responsibility. Without some form of measurement, a lot of the decisions that we would make would become significantly more challenging to line up with our goals. The issue isn't deciding what metrics should exist, but determining how much influence they have on the decisions we make.

This tension became a lot more noticeable when I started applying them to my own goals. I genuinely have a passion for learning, but I also understand that getting good grades can influence the opportunities that are available to me. Leadership positions can create opportunities for me to contribute meaningfully, but they can also be used as achievements that become something to compare and communicate. Even money, while crucial for independence and security, can quietly change from being a tool to support the life I want into the definition of being successful. I don't think I should ignore grades, extracurriculars, or financial benchmarks, because I know they can open many new doors for myself, but recognizing their effect on my goals has made me a lot more cautious about letting them define my progress.
